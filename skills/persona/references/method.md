# Persona method

## Where personas come from

Personas were popularised in interaction design by Alan Cooper (*The Inmates Are Running
the Asylum*, 1999) as archetypes built from observed behaviour. Pruitt and Grudin (2003)
showed how they help large teams share a common view of users, provided they are tied to
data. The method has well-known critics: personas built without evidence can be
misleading and hard to verify (Chapman & Milham, 2006). Since then, *data-driven*
personas have developed, using quantitative data and clustering alongside qualitative
work (Salminen et al., 2021, review 15 years of this research).

This skill keeps the best of both: the narrative power of personas, and full
traceability of every statement to its evidence.

## Confidence levels

| Level | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **High** | Supported by at least two independent sources, including at least one quantitative source, and consistent across them | "Buys mostly on mobile" (analytics + CRM) |
| **Medium** | Supported by one solid source, or by qualitative sources only | "Chooses for the short ingredient list" (6 interviews + reviews) |
| **Low** | Hypothesis: team belief, analogy, desk research on another market, or a single anecdote | "Would pay more for refills" (5 review mentions, no test) |

Also rate each persona as a whole: the share of its decision-relevant attributes that are
High or Medium.

## Source types

- **Quantitative**: CRM/order data, analytics, survey with enough respondents, ticket tags.
- **Qualitative**: interviews, reviews and verbatims, sales or support feedback,
  observation, Voice-of-the-Customer analysis.
- **Secondary**: published studies, panels, competitor analysis. Cite them precisely; never
  quote a figure you cannot source.
- **Assumption**: anything else. It is fine, as long as it is labelled.

## Finding behavioural variables

1. List candidate variables from the decision at stake (for a CRM plan: purchase frequency,
   channel, response to promotions; for a product launch: needs, expertise, barriers).
2. Place each observation (interviewee, segment, verbatim cluster) on each variable,
   e.g. price sensitivity: low / medium / high.
3. Look for observations that move together on several variables: these clusters are
   candidate personas.
4. With quantitative data, a simple segmentation (RFM, product mix, channel) or a clustering
   can confirm the clusters and give each persona a size.
5. Check that each persona would receive different marketing actions; merge otherwise.

## Useful lenses

- **Jobs to be done**: what "job" the customer hires the product for (functional, emotional,
  social), see Christensen et al., *Know Your Customers' "Jobs to Be Done"*, Harvard Business
  Review, 2016.
- **Primary / secondary / negative personas**: the primary persona is the one the offer must
  satisfy first; the negative persona prevents spending on the wrong audience.

## B2B adaptation

In B2B, distinguish the buying roles (user, influencer, decision-maker, buyer, gatekeeper)
and the company context (size, sector, maturity). One account often involves several
personas.
